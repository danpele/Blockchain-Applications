Today's Question and Route

Slide 1 in the PDF

A stablecoin promises one dollar, and the lecture asks how far and how often it misses that promise, and whether a miss can be seen coming. The question matters because stablecoins are the settlement asset of most crypto trades and the main collateral in decentralised finance (DeFi). A lending protocol that values a stablecoin at its market price will liquidate positions when that price falls, so a deviation of a few basis points (bp) can force sales that cannot be reversed.

The route has three parts. The first measures the peg: deviations in basis points, the tails of their distribution, episodes of stress, and the Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) weekend of March 2023 as a case. The second looks at the place where stablecoin prices are formed on chain, the automated market maker (AMM), and compares two of its pricing rules, the constant product and StableSwap. The third evaluates early-warning systems: how to judge an alarm, what the paper's alarm achieves, and what a simple price-only alarm achieves on our own data.

The data are daily (2021–2026) and hourly (2022–2026) prices of USDT, USDC and DAI from the data vendor EOD Historical Data (EODHD), and the stablecoin supply from DefiLlama on 21 October 2026.

From Lecture 3: One Depeg, Three Measurements

Slide 2 in the PDF

Lecture 3 summarised risk by one daily quantile. The value-at-risk at level 1%, VaR 1%, is minus the 1% quantile of daily changes, \(\mathrm{VaR}\,1\% = -q_{0.01}\), written as a positive loss. For USDC, measured in deviations from one dollar on daily closes from 2021 to 2026, the 1% quantile is \(q_{0.01} = -7.6\) bp, so VaR 1% is 7.6 bp: on 1% of days the closing price was at least 7.6 bp below one dollar.

The SVB weekend shows what this number misses. Measured on hourly USDC prices, the event has three separate dimensions. Its magnitude was a lowest hourly close of −1,036 bp at 07:00 Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) on 11 March 2023, while the lowest daily close was only −285 bp. Its duration was 62 hours from the first hour at or below −15 bp to the return above −15 bp, of which 44 hours were at or below −100 bp. The third dimension is the event probability: how often such episodes start, and whether the next 24 hours will contain one.

A daily VaR answers none of the three. It is one quantile of daily closes, so it says nothing about the depth of losses beyond it, the length of a depeg, or the time of the next one. The lecture measures all three: deviations and their tails, episodes, and an early-warning alarm.

What a Stablecoin Promises

Before measuring deviations from the peg, the analyst needs to know what holds the peg and what can break it. The section describes the three designs of stablecoins, the arbitrage that keeps the price near one dollar, the issuers that dominate the market, and three distinctions that decide which kind of depeg risk can be monitored at all.

Mechanisms

Slide 7 in the PDF

A stablecoin is a token that targets a fixed price \(P^\ast\) in a reference asset; in this lecture \(P^\ast = 1\) USD. Three designs exist. A fiat-backed stablecoin, such as USDT or USDC, is backed by reserves in cash and Treasury bills held by the issuer, which creates (mints) new tokens and buys them back (redeems them) at par, one dollar per token, for eligible clients. A crypto-backed stablecoin, such as DAI and its successor USDS, is created through over-collateralised loans in a smart contract: a user locks crypto assets worth more than the tokens borrowed, and the contract liquidates the collateral if its value falls too far, which protects the peg. An algorithmic stablecoin has no external collateral; TerraUSD, which collapsed in May 2022, relied on a swap that minted and burned it against a volatile token.

In all three designs the market price is held near the peg by arbitrage. If the price \(P_t\) is below one dollar, a trader buys the token on the market and redeems it with the issuer at one dollar; if it is above one dollar, the trader mints at one dollar and sells on the market. Both trades push the price back towards the peg.

Arbitrage needs three conditions. It needs access, because redemption is open only to clients who have passed the issuer's know-your-customer (KYC) checks and often only above a minimum size. It needs time, because redemptions are paid through banks, which work only during banking hours. And it needs trust that the reserves are there. When one of the three fails, nothing pulls the market price back, and it can leave the peg.

In brief

  • A stablecoin is a token that targets a fixed price \(P^\ast\) in a reference asset, here \(P^\ast = 1\) USD
  • Fiat-backed (USDT, USDC): reserves in cash and Treasury bills; the issuer mints and redeems at par for eligible clients
  • Crypto-backed (DAI/USDS): over-collateralised loans in a smart contract; liquidations protect the peg
  • Algorithmic (TerraUSD, May 2022): a mint/burn swap against a volatile token; no external collateral
  • The peg is held by arbitrage: if \(P_t < 1\), buy on the market and redeem at 1; if \(P_t > 1\), mint at 1 and sell
    • arbitrage needs access (know-your-customer (KYC) rules, minimum size), time (bank hours) and trust in the reserves
    • when one of the three fails, the market price can leave the peg

Three Distinctions

Slide 9 in the PDF

Three distinctions decide what kind of depeg risk is being measured. The first is between a fundamental and a market-defined peg. The fundamental value is the redemption value, what the issuer pays per token; the market-defined value is the price on a given trading venue. A token has no single market price: on 10 October 2025 USDe traded at 0.65 USD on one exchange while other venues stayed near one dollar.

The second distinction is between exogenous and endogenous causes. Exogenous causes, such as losses on the reserves, regulation or sanctions, arrive as news and are hard to predict from market data. Endogenous causes, such as a shortage of liquidity or one-sided order flow, arise inside the market and are visible on the blockchain before the price moves.

The third distinction is between transient and persistent depegs. A transient depeg lasts hours and is closed by arbitrage; a persistent one reflects a loss of confidence, as with TerraUSD. A transient depeg is not harmless, because liquidations executed at a bad price are irreversible. The lecture concentrates on market-defined, mostly endogenous and often transient risk, the kind for which monitoring is feasible.

In brief

  • Fundamental vs market-defined peg
    • fundamental: the redemption value; market-defined: the price on a given venue
    • a token has no single price: on 10 October 2025 USDe traded at 0.65 USD on one exchange while other venues stayed near 1
  • Exogenous vs endogenous
    • exogenous: reserve losses, regulation, sanctions; arrive as news, hard to predict
    • endogenous: liquidity and order flow inside the market; visible on chain before the price moves
  • Transient vs persistent
    • transient: hours, closed by arbitrage; persistent: a loss of confidence (TerraUSD)
    • a transient depeg is not harmless: liquidations at a bad price are irreversible
  • This lecture: market-defined, mostly endogenous, often transient risk, where monitoring is feasible

Section summary

A stablecoin holds its peg through arbitrage that needs access, time and trust in the reserves. The supply is dominated by two fiat-backed issuers, and the risk that can be monitored from market and on-chain data is the market-defined, endogenous and often transient depeg.

Measuring the Peg

The section turns the peg into numbers. It defines the deviation in basis points, the share of time beyond a threshold and the episode, then describes the daily distribution of the three dollars, its tails, and the SVB weekend hour by hour, with a worked example on how large the SVB discount should have been.

Definitions

Slide 10 in the PDF

The basic measure is the deviation of the price from the peg, expressed in basis points (1 bp = 0.01%). For a token with price \(P_t\) in USD, and for the price of USDC in USDT,

\[d_t = 10^4\,(P_t - 1), \qquad d_t^{\,\mathrm{USDC/USDT}} = 10^4\left(\frac{P_t^{\mathrm{USDC}}}{P_t^{\mathrm{USDT}}} - 1\right)\]
(4.1)

A price of 0.9985 USD gives \(d_t = -15\) bp. Two prices can be used for each interval: the closing price, and the intraday low, which is the lowest price reported during the interval, aggregated over the venues covered by the data vendor. Whether a given amount could actually have traded at the low depends on the venue, its depth and the size of the trade, so the low is an upper bound on stress, not a price an investor could count on.

The frequency of large deviations is measured by the exceedance probability, the share of observations with an absolute deviation of at least \(x\), and the breach indicator for a threshold \(\delta\), here 15 bp:

\[\hat P(|d| \geq x) = \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{1}\{|d_t| \geq x\}, \qquad b_t = \mathbb{1}\{|d_t| \geq \delta\}\]
(4.2)

where \(T\) is the number of observations and \(\mathbb{1}\{\cdot\}\) equals 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise.

Breach hours come in clusters, so they are not independent observations. The lecture therefore counts episodes: an episode starts at a breach hour that is preceded by 24 observed hours without a breach. A missing hour is never counted as quiet. A breach that follows a gap in the data is ambiguous, because a breach could have occurred in the missing hours, and it is set aside rather than counted as a new episode.

In brief

  • Deviation in basis points (1 bp = 0.01%):
    \[ d_t = 10^4\,(P_t - 1), \qquad d_t^{\,\mathrm{USDC/USDT}} = 10^4\left(\frac{P_t^{\mathrm{USDC}}}{P_t^{\mathrm{USDT}}} - 1\right) \]
    • closing price vs intraday low: the low is the lowest price reported in the interval, aggregated over venues
    • whether a given amount could trade at that price depends on the venue, its depth and the trade size
  • Exceedance probability and breach indicator, threshold \(\delta\) (here 15 bp):
    \[ \hat P(|d| \geq x) = \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb 1\{|d_t| \geq x\}, \qquad b_t = \mathbb 1\{|d_t| \geq \delta\} \]
  • An episode starts at a breach hour preceded by 24 observed hours without a breach
    • hours inside an episode are not independent observations: count episodes, not hours
    • a missing hour is never counted as quiet: a breach after a gap is ambiguous and is set aside

The SVB Weekend, Hour by Hour (own)

Slide 14 in the PDF

In March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) failed, and Circle, the issuer of USDC, disclosed that 3.3 billion USD of its reserves were deposited there. The chart follows the hourly deviations of USDC, DAI and USDT from one dollar, and the price of USDC in USDT (dashed red line), from 8 to 18 March.

USDC reached its lowest hourly value, −1,036 bp, at 07:00 UTC on Saturday 11 March; DAI reached −955 bp in the same hour. USDT moved the other way and traded above one dollar: holders who left USDC moved to the other dollar token. USDC returned above −100 bp at 22:00 UTC on Sunday 12 March, just before the US Federal Reserve (Fed), the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) and the Treasury announced a backstop for SVB's depositors, and above −15 bp only at 14:00 UTC on Monday 13 March.

DAI followed USDC almost one for one because a large part of its collateral was USDC. A loss on one stablecoin's reserves therefore passed to a second stablecoin through its balance sheet, a form of contagion.

Worked Example: Was the SVB Discount Fair?

Slide 15 in the PDF

A simple calculation asks whether the reserve loss alone explains the fall of USDC. Circle disclosed 3.3 billion USD of about 40 billion USD of reserves at SVB, a share \(s = 3.3/40 = 8.25\%\). If a fraction \(R\) of the SVB deposit is recovered (the recovery rate), the reserves behind each token are worth, in USD,

\[V(R) = 1 - s\,(1 - R)\]
(4.3)

Even with zero recovery, \(V(0) = 1 - 0.0825 = 0.9175\), a deviation of −825 bp. The market reached −1,036 bp, 211 bp below the value under zero recovery, the worst possible outcome for the SVB deposit.

These 211 bp are the part of the discount that this reserve-loss scenario does not explain; they are not an estimate of a liquidity premium. Several components are possible, and the calculation does not separate them. Time: banks were closed over the weekend, so nobody could redeem at par. Uncertainty: about the size of the loss and about the issuer's other reserve holdings. Frictions: limited arbitrage capital, the restriction of redemption to approved clients, and trading spread over many venues. A peg can therefore fail even when the reserves cover 92% or more of the tokens: access to redemption matters as much as the backing.

In brief

  • Circle disclosed 3.3 bn USD of about 40 bn USD reserves at SVB: share \(s = 3.3/40 = 8.25\%\)
  • With recovery rate \(R\) on the SVB deposit, the redemption value per token is
    \[ V(R) = 1 - s\,(1 - R), \qquad V(0) = 0.9175 \;\Rightarrow\; d = -825 \text{ bp} \]

Where the Price Is Formed: AMMs

On a blockchain, stablecoins are traded mostly in automated market makers (AMMs): smart contracts that hold two or more tokens and quote a price from a fixed formula, the invariant, instead of an order book. The section compares the constant-product rule with the StableSwap rule used for stablecoins, and shows why the price in such a pool can stay flat while the pool is running out of one token.

Constant-Product AMM

Slide 16 in the PDF

A constant-product pool holds an amount \(x\) of token X and \(y\) of token Y, and every trade must keep the product of the two balances fixed, \(x\,y = k\). The marginal price of X in units of Y is \(p = y/x\). A trader who sells \(\Delta x\) of X to the pool raises the balance of X to \(x + \Delta x\), and the pool pays out enough Y to keep the product at \(k\):

\[\Delta y = y - \frac{k}{x + \Delta x} = \frac{y\,\Delta x}{x + \Delta x}, \qquad \text{slippage} = 1 - \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta x}{x + \Delta x} \quad (p = 1)\]
(4.4)

The slippage is the percentage by which the trader receives less than the marginal price before the trade; the second formula holds for a pool that starts at price 1.

In the worked example the pool holds 10 million X and 10 million Y, and a trader sells 1 million X. The pool pays \(\Delta y = 10 \times 1/11 = 0.909\) million Y, a slippage of 9.1%. For two tokens that should both be worth one dollar, a loss of 9.1% on a trade of one tenth of the pool is very large. The constant-product rule spreads liquidity over all prices from 0 to infinity, so it never runs out of either token, but most of that liquidity sits at prices that a stable pair should never reach.

In brief

  • A pool holds \(x\) of token X and \(y\) of token Y; trades keep the invariant \(x\,y = k\)
  • Marginal price of X in Y: \(p = y/x\). Selling \(\Delta x\) returns
    \[ \Delta y = y - \frac{k}{x + \Delta x} = \frac{y\,\Delta x}{x + \Delta x}, \qquad \text{slippage} = 1 - \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta x}{x + \Delta x}\;\; (p = 1) \]
  • Worked example: pool 10 m X + 10 m Y, sell 1 m X
    \[ \Delta y = \frac{10 \cdot 1}{11} = 0.909 \text{ m}, \qquad \text{slippage } 9.1\% \]
  • Liquidity is spread over all prices from 0 to \(\infty\): robust but very expensive for stable pairs

StableSwap (Curve): Flat Near Parity

Slide 17 in the PDF

The StableSwap invariant of Egorov (2019), used by the exchange Curve, combines a constant sum with a constant product. For \(n\) coins with balances \(x_i\) and an amplification parameter \(A\),

\[A\,n^n \sum_i x_i + D = A\,D\,n^n + \frac{D^{n+1}}{n^n \prod_i x_i}\]
(4.5)

where \(D\) is the total balance of the pool when it is balanced at price 1, that is when every \(x_i = D/n\). The parameter \(A\) moves the curve between two extremes. As \(A \to 0\) the invariant becomes the constant product; as \(A \to \infty\) it becomes the constant sum \(x + y = D\), where the price is exactly 1 whatever the balances.

For a sale of 1 million X the slippage is 9.09% under the constant product, 0.48% with \(A = 10\), 0.05% with \(A = 100\) and 0.003% with \(A = 2{,}000\): near balance StableSwap is thousands of times cheaper. The advantage shrinks as the sale grows. For a sale of 9.5 million, which almost empties the pool of Y, the slippage with \(A = 100\) is 2.94%, nearly sixty times its value for a sale of 1 million.

Early Warning

An early-warning system turns data into an alarm that should ring before a depeg, not during it. The section sets up the prediction problem, shows how the choice of the threshold changes the number of events, explains why the usual classification metrics are the wrong yardstick for an alarm, and compares the results of the paper with a price-only alarm estimated for this lecture.

From Pool State to a Warning: the Set-up

Slide 19 in the PDF

Each hour \(t\), a vector of features \(\mathbf x_t\) is computed from data available up to hour \(t\) only. Three examples show the idea. The balance of the Curve 3pool, a StableSwap pool of DAI, USDC and USDT, is summarised by the entropy of the three shares, which equals \(\ln 3 = 1.0986\) when the pool is balanced and falls as it becomes one-sided (Appendix). The depth of the Uniswap V3 pool is measured by the liquidity available within a few price steps (ticks) of the current price. The recent deviation enters as \(|d_t|\) and as its maximum over the last 24 hours.

The event to predict is a breach, \(|d_{t+h}| \geq 15\) bp at some hour \(h\) within the next 24 hours, which defines the label \(y_t \in \{0, 1\}\). The data are split in time order: the models are fitted on the early years, the alert threshold is chosen on a later validation year, and the results are reported once, on a final test period that was not used for any choice.

Two metrics appear in the results. Precision is the share of alerted hours that are followed by a breach; recall is the share of positive hours, those followed by a breach, that are alerted. Lowering the alert threshold raises recall and usually lowers precision. The area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) summarises this trade-off over all thresholds: a random score obtains the base rate, the share of positive hours (5.9% in the paper), and a perfect score obtains 1. The paper uses further features (Gegenbauer scores of the liquidity curve, ownership of liquidity positions, data from the lending protocol Aave) that summarise the same pools; they are defined in the Appendix.

Evaluating an Alarm, Not a Classifier

Slide 22 in the PDF

The usual metrics of classification are computed row by row, hour by hour. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) is the probability that a randomly chosen positive hour gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative hour; the AUPRC was defined above. Both reward a model for labelling hours correctly during an episode, when the deviation is already large and easy to see. An alert raised after the breach has begun is no warning, but it raises the AUC.

The paper therefore evaluates the alarm at the level of events. An alert is an hour whose score is above a threshold; alerts closer than 24 hours (a refractory period) are merged into one alert episode. An event \(e\) that starts at time \(\tau_e\) is detected if an alert falls in the window from 24 hours to 1 hour before it, \([\tau_e - 24\text{h}, \tau_e - 1\text{h}]\), and its lead time \(\ell_e\) is the number of hours between the first valid alert and \(\tau_e\). All other alert episodes are false alerts, and \(F_m\) is their number per month.

The two sides are combined in a utility:

\[U = \frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{e \in E} v(\ell_e) - 0.05\,F_m, \qquad v(\ell) = 0.10 + 0.90\,\frac{\ell - 1}{23} \;\text{ for } 1 \leq \ell \leq 24, \text{ else } 0\]
(4.7)

where \(E\) is the set of events and \(|E|\) their number. A detected event earns between 0.10, for a lead of 1 hour, and 1, for a lead of 24 hours; a missed event earns 0; each false alert per month costs 0.05. The alert threshold is chosen on the preceding validation fold, to maximise \(U\) subject to at most 2 false alerts per month on validation. It is never re-tuned on the test fold, where \(F_m\) can therefore exceed 2.

In brief

  • Row-level area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) reward correct labels during an episode; an alert after the breach is no warning
  • Event-level protocol
    • alerts: hours with score above a threshold; a 24-hour refractory period merges them into alert episodes
    • event \(e\) at time \(\tau_e\) is detected if an alert falls in \([\tau_e - 24\text{h}, \tau_e - 1\text{h}]\); lead \(\ell_e\) = hours before \(\tau_e\)
    • all other alert episodes are false; \(F_m\) = false alerts per month
  • Utility

Worked Example: Computing the Utility

Slide 23 in the PDF

The chart draws the credit \(v(\ell)\) of formula (4.7) against the lead time: zero for a lead below 1 hour, a jump to 0.10 at 1 hour, a straight rise to 1 at 24 hours, and zero again for alerts more than 24 hours ahead, which are too early to count as a warning of this event.

Consider four events. The first valid alerts come 14 hours, 2 hours, never, and 20 hours before the breach, and the alarm produces 2.5 false alerts per month. The credits are \(v(14) = 0.10 + 0.90 \times 13/23 = 0.609\), \(v(2) = 0.139\), 0 for the missed event and \(v(20) = 0.843\). Their mean is \(\bar v = 0.398\), and the utility is \(U = 0.398 - 0.05 \times 2.5 = 0.273\).

The weights have a simple reading. One more false alert per month costs 0.05 of utility, about the value of detecting one extra event out of four with a 2-hour lead. A user who finds false alerts more or less costly than this would choose a different penalty, and the ranking of alarms could change.

A Threshold Autoregression

Slide 31 in the PDF

If arbitrage costs \(c\) per dollar, deviations smaller than \(c\) are not worth trading and may persist, while larger ones should close quickly. A threshold autoregressive (TAR) model captures this idea by letting the speed of reversion differ inside and outside a band \(\pm\gamma\):

\[\Delta d_t = \mu + \rho_{in}\, d_{t-1}\,\mathbb{1}\{|d_{t-1}| \leq \gamma\} + \rho_{out}\, d_{t-1}\,\mathbb{1}\{|d_{t-1}| > \gamma\} + \varepsilon_t\]
(4.8)

The band hypothesis is \(\rho_{in} \approx 0\) and \(\rho_{out} < 0\); a coefficient \(\rho\) gives a half-life of \(\ln 0.5/\ln(1+\rho)\) hours. For each \(\gamma\) on a grid the model is estimated by ordinary least squares (OLS), and \(\hat\gamma\) minimises the sum of squared residuals (SSR). The test of a threshold effect, \(H_0: \rho_{in} = \rho_{out}\), is non-standard because \(\gamma\) has no meaning under \(H_0\) (Hansen, 1996). The statistic is the largest Wald statistic over the grid, \(\sup W\), with a heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC) variance, and its p-value comes from a fixed-regressor bootstrap with \(B = 199\) replications.

In brief

  • If arbitrage costs \(c\), small deviations are not worth trading; beyond \(c\) they are. A threshold autoregressive (AR) model captures this:
    \[ \Delta d_t = \mu + \rho_{in}\, d_{t-1}\,\mathbb 1\{|d_{t-1}| \leq \gamma\} + \rho_{out}\, d_{t-1}\,\mathbb 1\{|d_{t-1}| > \gamma\} + \varepsilon_t \]
    • band hypothesis: \(\rho_{in} \approx 0\), \(\rho_{out} < 0\); half-life \(h = \ln 0.5 / \ln(1 + \rho)\)
  • Estimation: for each \(\gamma\) on a grid, ordinary least squares (OLS); \(\hat\gamma = \arg\min_\gamma \mathrm{SSR}(\gamma)\), the sum of squared residuals (SSR)
  • Test of a threshold effect, \(H_0: \rho_{in} = \rho_{out}\); \(\gamma\) is not identified under \(H_0\), so the usual \(\chi^2\) fails; with a heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC) variance:
    \[ \sup W = \sup_{\gamma \in \Gamma} W(\gamma), \qquad W(\gamma) = \frac{(\hat\rho_{in} - \hat\rho_{out})^2}{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{HC}}(\hat\rho_{in} - \hat\rho_{out})} \]
  • p-value by the fixed-regressor bootstrap (Hansen (1996)): \(y_t^\ast = \hat e_t z_t\), \(z_t \sim N(0,1)\), recompute \(\sup W\), \(B = 199\)

Uniswap V3: Concentrated Liquidity

Slide 35 in the PDF

In Uniswap V3 (Adams et al., 2021) liquidity providers choose the price range in which their funds are used. Prices sit on a grid of ticks \(p_k = 1.0001^k\), so one tick is 1 bp. The active liquidity \(L_{t,j}\) at tick \(j\) is the sum of all positions whose range contains \(j\), and the curve \(j \mapsto L_{t,j}\) changes every hour. The paper sums it from the current tick \(k_t\) upwards, \(Q_t^+(k) = \sum_{j=k_t}^{k} L_{t,j}\), and downwards, \(Q_t^-(k) = \sum_{j=k}^{k_t} L_{t,j}\), a proxy for depth that is not yet a token amount; crossing tick \(j\) upwards costs \(L_{t,j}(\sqrt{p_{j+1}} - \sqrt{p_j})\) of Y, and downwards \(L_{t,j}(1/\sqrt{p_j} - 1/\sqrt{p_{j+1}})\) of X. The area under each curve measures depth on one side, the slope at \(k_t\) the local depth, and a sudden flat part is a liquidity cliff, where a small trade moves the price a lot. The data come hourly from The Graph subgraphs, ±50 ticks around the peg; positions minted and burned in the same block are just-in-time (JIT) liquidity and may not be there under stress.

In brief

  • Liquidity providers choose a price range; prices live on ticks \(p_k = 1.0001^k\), one tick = 1 bp
  • Active liquidity at tick \(j\): \(L_{t,j}\) = sum of all positions whose range contains \(j\); the curve \(j \mapsto L_{t,j}\) changes every hour
  • Cumulative liquidity proxy from the current tick \(k_t\) (Chaffard, Găman, Staures, Ginavar, Jheng, Härdle & Pele (2026)), not yet a token amount:
    \[ Q_t^+(k) = \sum_{j = k_t}^{k} L_{t,j}\;\; (k > k_t), \qquad Q_t^-(k) = \sum_{j = k}^{k_t} L_{t,j}\;\; (k < k_t) \]
    • token amounts to cross tick \(j\) (price \(p = y/x\)): up, pay \(\Delta y_j = L_{t,j}(\sqrt{p_{j+1}} - \sqrt{p_j})\) of Y; down, pay \(\Delta x_j = L_{t,j}(1/\sqrt{p_j} - 1/\sqrt{p_{j+1}})\) of X
    • area under the curves: global depth on each side; slope at \(k_t\): local depth
    • a sudden flat part: a liquidity cliff, where a small trade moves the price a lot
  • Data: The Graph subgraphs, hourly, \(\pm 50\) ticks around the peg; positions minted and burned in the same block are just-in-time (JIT) liquidity and may not be there under stress

A Fixed Basis for a Moving Curve

Slide 37 in the PDF

To turn the liquidity curve into a few numbers, the paper maps prices to a peg-centred coordinate \(x \in [-1, 1]\), with the peg at \(x = 0\), takes the log-liquidity \(\ell_t(x) = \log\{L_t(x) + 1\}\), and expands it in Gegenbauer polynomials \(\phi_k^{(\alpha)}\), which are orthonormal under the weight \(w_\alpha(x) = (1 - x^2)^{\alpha - 1/2}\): \(\ell_t(x) \approx \sum_{k=0}^{K} \beta_{t,k}\,\phi_k^{(\alpha)}(x)\). The coefficient \(\beta_{t,0}\) measures the level of liquidity, \(\beta_{t,1}\) the asymmetry between the two sides, \(\beta_{t,2}\) the concentration at the peg, and higher orders local notches and cliffs; odd orders are asymmetric and even orders symmetric. Their sums of squares, \(E_t^{odd}\) over odd \(k\) and \(E_t^{even}\) over even \(k \geq 2\), are the energies. The parameter \(\alpha\) moves weight between the centre and the edges and is tuned as a hyperparameter. A fixed basis keeps the scores comparable over time, unlike a rolling functional principal component analysis (FPCA), whose components change with the window.

In brief

  • Peg-centred coordinate \(x \in [-1, 1]\) (peg at \(x = 0\)), log-liquidity \(\ell_t(x) = \log\{L_t(x) + 1\}\)
  • Expansion in Gegenbauer polynomials, orthonormal under the weight \(w_\alpha\):
    \[ \ell_t(x) \approx \sum_{k=0}^{K} \beta_{t,k}\, \phi_k^{(\alpha)}(x), \qquad w_\alpha(x) = (1 - x^2)^{\alpha - 1/2}, \qquad \beta_{t,k} = \int_{-1}^{1} \ell_t(x)\,\phi_k^{(\alpha)}(x)\,w_\alpha(x)\,dx \]
  • Reading the coefficients
    • \(k = 0\): level of liquidity; \(k = 1\): left-right asymmetry; \(k = 2\): concentration at the peg
    • higher \(k\): local notches and cliffs; odd \(k\) asymmetric, even \(k\) symmetric
  • Energies: \(E_t^{odd} = \sum_{k \text{ odd}} \beta_{t,k}^2\), \(\;E_t^{even} = \sum_{k \geq 2 \text{ even}} \beta_{t,k}^2\)
  • \(\alpha\) moves the weight between centre and edges; it is tuned as a hyperparameter. A fixed basis keeps the scores comparable over time, unlike rolling functional principal component analysis (FPCA)

From an Alarm to a Distribution

Slide 40 in the PDF

An alarm says whether a breach is likely; a risk manager also needs how far the price may go, for example the probability of crossing a liquidation level or the expected shortfall (ES) of a depeg, the mean loss beyond a quantile. The paper therefore forecasts the whole conditional distribution of \(d_{t+h}\) for \(h = 1, \dots, 24\) hours. Its neural network has one branch for the history of the target and one for the covariates; it selects a few covariates and returns a quantile function for each horizon. It is compared with five benchmarks: a naive forecast, an autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model, a generalised autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity model with Student-t errors (GARCH-t), and two neural models, the Time-series Dense Encoder (TiDE) and TimeXer, which receive the same distributional output and loss. The test period runs from January 2025 to May 2026.

In brief

  • An alarm says whether; a risk manager also needs how far: the probability of crossing a liquidation level, the expected shortfall (ES) of a depeg
  • The paper forecasts the full conditional distribution of \(d_{t+h}\), \(h = 1, \dots, 24\)
  • Architecture, in brief
    • two branches: the target history (dilated causal convolutions) and the covariates (one token per variable)

A Quantile Function with Pareto Tails

Slide 41 in the PDF

The forecast distribution is written as a quantile function \(Q_t(u)\), the value below which the deviation falls with probability \(u\). Its body, between the levels \(\tau_L\) and \(\tau_U\), is a monotone spline centred at the median and scaled. Below \(\tau_L\) and above \(\tau_U\) it is replaced by the tails of a generalised Pareto distribution (GPD), joined smoothly to the body. Each tail has a shape parameter, \(\xi_L\) and \(\xi_U\); a larger \(\xi\) means a heavier tail, and for \(\xi \geq 1/2\) the tail has infinite variance (Lecture 2: the tail index is \(1/\xi\)). Separate parameters allow depegs of USDC below USDT and of USDT below USDC to differ.

In brief

  • Body: monotone I-spline, centred at the median and scaled
    \[ Q_t^0(u) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} a_{t,k} I_k(u),\; a_{t,k} > 0; \qquad Q_t^B(u) = \ell_t + \frac{s_t}{\sqrt{V_t}}\,\{Q_t^0(u) - Q_t^0(0.5)\} \]
  • Tails: generalised Pareto distribution (GPD), joined smoothly at \(\tau_L\) and \(\tau_U\)
    \[ Q_t(u) = \begin{cases} z_L - \dfrac{\beta_L}{\xi_L}\Big[\big(\tfrac{u}{\tau_L}\big)^{-\xi_L} - 1\Big], & u < \tau_L \\[2mm] Q_t^B(u), & \tau_L \leq u \leq \tau_U \\[1mm] z_U + \dfrac{\beta_U}{\xi_U}\Big[\big(\tfrac{1-u}{1-\tau_U}\big)^{-\xi_U} - 1\Big], & u > \tau_U \end{cases} \]
  • \(z_L = Q^B_t(\tau_L)\), \(\beta_L = \tau_L\,Q_t^{B\prime}(\tau_L)\) and symmetrically for the upper tail; separate \(\xi_L\), \(\xi_U\) allow USDC and USDT depegs to differ
  • For \(\xi \geq 1/2\) the tail has infinite variance (Lecture 2: tail index \(1/\xi\))

Scoring a Distribution: CRPS and twCRPS

Slide 43 in the PDF

A forecast distribution \(F\) is scored against the outcome \(y\) by the continuous ranked probability score (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007),

\[\mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \big(F(x) - \mathbb{1}\{y \leq x\}\big)^2 dx = 2\int_0^1 \rho_u\big(y - Q(u)\big)\,du\]
(4.9)

the squared gap between the forecast cumulative distribution function (CDF) and the step function of the outcome, shaded in the left panel; lower is better. It equals twice the average of the pinball loss \(\rho_u(e) = e\,(u - \mathbb{1}\{e < 0\})\) over all quantile levels. The threshold-weighted CRPS (twCRPS) of Gneiting & Ranjan (2011) applies a weight \(w(r)\) that is close to 0 near the peg and close to 1 beyond \(\pm\delta\) (right panel, \(\delta = 15\) bp, smoothing \(h = 2\)), so the score concentrates on the depeg region. The paper trains its model on twCRPS and evaluates it on both scores.

In brief

  • Continuous ranked probability score (Gneiting & Raftery (2007)), a proper scoring rule:
    \[ \mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \big(F(x) - \mathbb 1\{y \leq x\}\big)^2 dx = 2\int_0^1 \rho_u\big(y - Q(u)\big)\,du \]
    • \(\rho_u(e) = e\,(u - \mathbb 1\{e < 0\})\): the pinball loss; CRPS averages it over all quantiles
    • Normal: \(\mathrm{CRPS}(N(\mu, \sigma^2), y) = \sigma\big[z(2\Phi(z) - 1) + 2\varphi(z) - 1/\sqrt\pi\big]\), \(z = (y - \mu)/\sigma\)
  • Threshold-weighted CRPS (Gneiting & Ranjan (2011)): put the weight where depegs are
    \[ \mathrm{twCRPS} = 2\int_0^1 \rho_u\big(v(y) - v(Q(u))\big)\,du, \quad v(r) = \int_0^r w(s)\,ds, \quad w(r) = \sigma\Big(\frac{r - \delta}{h}\Big) + \sigma\Big(\frac{-r - \delta}{h}\Big) \]
    • \(\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\), the logistic function; \(h\) sets how sharply the weight rises around \(\pm\delta\)
  • The paper uses twCRPS as training loss and both scores for evaluation

Calibration and Ablation

Slide 46 in the PDF

A forecast is calibrated if the outcomes look like draws from it. The probability integral transform (PIT) \(u_t = \hat F_t(y_t)\) should then be uniform on \([0, 1]\), with mean 0.5 and variance \(1/12 = 0.0833\); the paper finds 0.5022 and 0.0878, and a Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) distance of 0.0288 with a 48-hour block-bootstrap p-value of 0.079, so we do not reject uniformity at 5%. VaR exceedances are too frequent at the 2–5% levels in both tails and close to nominal at 0.5–1%, and the forecast ES is more extreme than the realised one, a conservative error. The chart reports an ablation, the loss in the 1% and 99% quantile (pinball) losses (QL) when one part of the model is removed: a Gaussian output instead of the spline with Pareto tails costs 44% at the 1% quantile, and dropping the liquidity features costs 25% at the 99% quantile, more than dropping all covariates (14%).
